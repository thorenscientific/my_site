# Other Matter (Front, back, etc.)






**Personal Statement**

Between my earliest memories and this moment, there is no perceptible step change in my interests. I’ve wandered between various hobbies, projects, and job titles, but the overarching theme has been: science and engineering. For its own sake, because it’s fun, and because in the end it’s a net benefit to society.

One of my earliest memories is of being in my Dad’s garage, with him showing me a contraption he had just built out of a timer and a few pilot lights. I remember the buzz of the timer, and lights coming on in succession - one light, two lights, then…. BOOM! Blown fuse.

Dad wasn’t an engineer, but he had various technician level jobs throughout his life. He’d always bring home dead equipment to fix up or take apart, and I’ve still got random parts from those days that show up in a drawer or box somewhere. Childhood was filled with building, playing with, and fixing random stuff either on my own or with my Dad and brother - jacob’s ladders, carbon arc-lamps, crystal radios, stereo equipment, chemistry sets, and so on.

I wasn’t the best high school student. I got by and passed, but for some reason I never really “meshed” with either my teachers’ styles, or just the way things were taught, or maybe I just didn’t know how to work hard yet. I did, however, take all of the shop classes - drafting, metalworking, woodworking, power mechanics. In my junior year I dropped my pride and asked my chemistry teacher for a recommendation letter for my applications to University of Connecticut and University of Maine. I was accepted at both, and I chose University of Maine in Orono, majoring in bio-resource engineering, as a way to keep tuition down since it wasn’t offered in Connecticut.

My first class at UMaine was economics 101, taught by an old Eastern European professor, and I loved it. It was as if the switch had been finally flipped - I had the freedom to study and learn my own way. My first engineering class was a project-based, fairly non-technical course taught by another old professor and dean emeritus, Dr. Norman Smith. The class project centered around the logistics and economics of brewing enough beer for a big party at the annual Family and Friends event. That’s my kind of class! And it cemented the idea that engineering isn’t just science, it’s scheduling, economics, and interpersonal communication. The next four years were filled with the standard mechanical engineering curriculum - where I did fairly well - not perfect, but well above average. And those shop classes from high school? They gave me a nice head start in drafting, metallurgy, and machinery classes.

I interned at Parker Hannifin in my junior year, working on tiny valves for medical and instrumentation applications. Parker is a great company and the division I was in was fine, but after some time I found myself reviewing electronic designs for the electrical engineer, who was not terribly good, by his own admission. When the limitations of that job started to become clear, and I “just wasn’t feeling it” every day driving into the office, I started considering what to do next.

Parker had sponsored a First Robotics team, and I had volunteered for the 3 years I was there. I was in charge of the control system by default, since I was the most comfortable with electronics. The system used a “Basic Stamp” microcontroller from Parallax, which I had wanted to play with for a while, so I bought one and started working my way through the application notes. One of those notes showed how to read data from a Linear Technology LTC1298 analog to digital converter, so I bought a couple from DigiKey, built up the circuit, and loaded up the example code…

What happened next is easily in the top 5 most significant events of my life.

As I turned the little blue potentiometer on the breadboard, and watched the number on the blue, DOS 6.22 screen go from zero to 4095 (it’s a 12-bit ADC), back to zero, back up to 4095, something irreversibly snapped in my brain - “This is like a tiny little voltmeter, inside of a chip?? That’s the coolest thing I’ve ever seen!”

Indeed, there was no turning back. I almost immediately signed up for a class in assembly language at UMass Lowell, and around the same time one of my undergrad professors called me and told me about a research and teaching assistantship in the UMaine EE department. Without question - I jumped at the opportunity. I spent the next 2-½ years doing coursework, researching robust weather instrumentation, and perhaps most significantly for my future career, designing and running undergrad circuits and systems labs. As I worked my way through the program, I’d occasionally come across other Linear Tech components, and read the occasional application note. Toward the end, I saw an advertisement in EE Times (when it was still an oversized paper magazine), Linear Technology was looking for an applications engineer to support their first sigma-delta ADC, the LTC2400. My advisor immediately told me what a great company Linear was, and I sent in my resume.

I got a call back from the signal conditioning applications manager, Rich Markell, and we talked for probably half an hour. At the end he said “Well, you seem better suited for the mixed signal department, but if that doesn’t work let me know, we may have something in signal conditioning.” What followed was the standard gauntlet of phone interviews, a flight to San Jose for another 8 hours of grilling, at the end of which I was informed I'd be getting an offer (which I did, and accepted).

It’s been an extraordinarily fulfilling 23 years between then and now. Spending the first couple of years under considerable stress as I learned what it takes to support high-performance data converters, not only the technical aspects, but how to interact with customers - especially the difficult ones. How to translate between IC designers and field applications engineers. How to write application notes, how to really understand what a customer needs to solve their problem, beyond just a part number and a copy of a datasheet.

As my career progressed, I was promoted to applications manager and spent seven years in that role. Those were some tough, hard-working years, but also when my learning and personal growth took off - we ran very lean, so I acted as backup for almost all of the engineers on the team. As a manager I stayed very hands-on, developing and reviewing training content, taking phone calls when folks went on vacation, filling in gaps whenever needed. At some point I started talking to my supervisor and general manager about moving back to an individual contributor role. When one of the experimental product lines was dissolved, a perfectly good manager became available to take my place. After some quick negotiation, I made what I consider a lateral move to the role of staff scientist.

As staff scientist, I continued to provide high-level product support, and architected several data converter evaluation platforms. Without the management responsibilities, I focused on being more available as a mentor to new college grads, and as an advisor to, well, anyone that asked for advice. One constant annoyance, especially on the high-speed ADC and DAC front, was Analog Devices. As I was researching how ADI’s reference designs, software, and FPGA code was put together so well, I kept seeing the same few names over and over again. In 2018, Analog Devices officially acquired Linear Technology.

Another top-5 career moment was when (the late) Dave Babicz, director of Analog Devices’ System Development Group (SDG), took me aside in private and said “Pssst… why don’t you come join my team?”
Me: “Really? You know I’m not super great at any one thing, I’m sort of good at a lot of random things though.”
Dave: “Well, sometimes those kinds of people are pretty handy to have around.”

And with that I ended up joining SDG, on the same team with those same names that had represented an annoying competitor for so many years. In many ways I was doing the same type of work in SDG that I had been doing at Linear, but with a larger team of more experienced engineers by my side. It was quite intimidating at first; reminiscent of my first few years at Linear Tech. But like diving into Linear Tech, it was an opportunity to grow.

That brings me to the present. My work is fulfilling, and in addition to my “day job” responsibilities as a systems architect - getting reference designs out the door, writing documentation, developing training content - I have plenty of opportunity to teach, mentor, and learn on my own. I also find enough time to devote to another passion, ADI’s University program, consisting mainly of free, open-source educational content. I intentionally blur the lines between this program and material I develop for internal use - if there’s nothing proprietary and a student at some level might find it useful, I find a way to publish externally.

It’s only recently that I’ve been considering EITHER continuing education OR publishing an E-book. I plan on working in industry until I turn 60 (in 2033) after which I’ll gravitate toward  teaching in some capacity, perhaps at the university level. This work will serve as a mechanism to develop and formalize a body of work that is mutually beneficial to my university contacts, Analog Devices, and myself, and will expand the possibilities for my future work in academia.

I have a broad range of interests in electrical engineering and related fields - signal conditioning and signal chains, instrumentation, power electronics, radar, phased arrays, engineering and science education, experiential learning pedagogy to name a few. While that is a wide list, once the subject is chosen I will focus to an appropriate level of depth. I am flexible on who I want to work with - ideally I would work with some combination of an instructor teaching the subject matter, other graduate students, and Analog Devices colleagues.

**Success Factors**

I've had an eclectic educational and career path spanning mechanical, manufacturing, and electrical engineering. I entered the bio-resource engineering program at University of Maine as a way to keep costs down vs. mechanical engineering, as it wasn’t offered in my home state. It turned out to be a great decision otherwise; the department was small but rigorous, with excellent professors and a “you do and build everything” philosophy.

I was largely self-taught in electronics and was already doing electronic design at Parker Hannifin, working on valves and pressure controllers, but I felt I needed formal education to progress. When a combination research and teaching assistantship in the UMaine EE department came up, I jumped at the opportunity. I spent the next 2.5 years doing classwork, research on robust weather instrumentation, and teaching electronics and controls labs. My advisor gave me free rein to design the lab curriculum, an experience I’ve leveraged ever since.

My time at Linear Technology and Analog Devices has been incredible - amazing mentors, opportunities to dig deep into a wide array of circuits, systems, and applications, write about them, and teach others.

I’ve always pushed my boundaries, tried new things, experimented with alternative approaches to problems, figured out what works and what doesn’t, and perhaps most importantly, put considerable time into reflection and self-awareness about how I learn, work, and communicate. At this stage in my career, I’m well positioned to take a realistic, practical approach to integrating a PhD program into my work and personal life for the next couple of years. I live by the Pareto principle, whether bringing a product to production or working on a degree: identify risk factors early and often, adjust where necessary, keep my sights on the end goal at all times.

**Another "Success Factors" list**

- Work harder and smarter.
- Take responsibility, don't wait for it to be given to you.
- You were hired for what you can learn, not what you know. So learn FAST.
